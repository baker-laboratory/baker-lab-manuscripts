## Shared manuscript architecture

### Abstract or summary

Across journals, the dominant functional sequence is:

1. Define the capability or biological problem.
2. Identify the specific limitation in current methods, molecules, or natural systems.
3. State the design strategy and main contribution directly.
4. Give discriminating evidence: success rate, affinity, catalytic efficiency, structural agreement, selectivity, throughput, cellular response, or in vivo outcome.
5. End with the newly enabled capability and its bounded significance.

Use a few decisive quantitative results rather than listing every experiment. Preserve the denominator for every success rate. Do not merge computational pass rate, expression rate, binding hit rate, structural success, and functional success; they answer different questions.

### Introduction

The common logic is a short inverted pyramid:

1. Why the scientific capability matters.
2. What precise constraint prevents the desired outcome.
3. Why the proposed design principle could overcome that constraint.
4. What success must mean and how it will be evaluated.

`We reasoned that...`, `We sought to...`, and similar transitions work when the method follows causally from the limitation. Do not use them as decoration. The explicit IPD rule against previewing Results in the Baker-review Introduction overrides journal examples that briefly summarize results in the opening.

### Results

Organize result units around claims and figures, not experiment chronology. A recurring evidence ladder is:

1. Design principle, model, or target definition.
2. Computational generation, filtering, or benchmark.
3. Experimental screening or primary measurement.
4. Biophysical, structural, or mechanistic validation.
5. Generalization, cellular function, materials property, or in vivo application.

Not every paper contains every rung. A purely computational paper must not imply experimental validation. A biological application paper may begin with target biology before the design step.

Within each result unit:

1. State the question or design objective.
2. Give only the setup needed to interpret the result.
3. Report the data, including denominators and comparison conditions.
4. State the bounded inference.
5. Report the relevant failure, limitation, or exception near the claim it constrains.

Standalone `Results`, `Discussion`, or `Conclusion` headings vary by journal. Audit rhetorical functions rather than requiring a heading copied from another venue.

### Discussion or conclusion

The recurring sequence is:

1. State the achieved capability and strongest evidence.
2. Compare directly with the relevant prior method, natural molecule, or engineering baseline.
3. Explain what the result establishes and what it does not establish.
4. Name the most important limitation or boundary of generality.
5. End with a concrete next design step or enabled experiment.

Avoid replaying figures in order. Avoid generic final claims that the method will transform the field.

## Method and enzyme-design playbook

For an inference-time enzyme-design manuscript such as a method that optimizes protein backbones and sequences around a theozyme, route jointly to the method and enzyme-design comparators.

### Story questions

The manuscript should make the following explicit:

1. What is the designed object: backbone, sequence, active-site geometry, ligand pose, conformational ensemble, or a coupled combination?
2. What does the prior method hold fixed or fail to optimize?
3. What intervention is new: training, conditioning, inference-time guidance, sampling, scoring, filtering, or an iterative loop?
4. Which comparison isolates that intervention?
5. What evidence supports geometry, foldability, sequence compatibility, catalytic competence, and generality separately?

### Fair computational comparisons

- Match sampling budgets, input information, evaluation regions, structure-prediction protocol, and filters whenever possible.
- State every denominator, including generated, surviving, predicted, expressed, assayed, and successful designs.
- Separate best-of-budget coverage from per-sample efficiency.
- Report model version, checkpoint, inference schedule, random-seed policy, recycle/step count, template and MSA policy, and selection thresholds when they affect reproducibility.
- Explain whether hyperparameters were selected on the reported targets or on a separate development set.
- Use ablations to isolate each new component; do not use a larger sampling budget as evidence for an algorithmic gain.
- Include failure modes such as collapse, poor geometry, low survival, target-specific regressions, or lack of experimental validation.

### Enzyme-specific evidence boundaries

- Define the theozyme or catalytic motif when first used and cite the mechanistic source for the intended catalytic geometry.
- Distinguish preservation of motif geometry from evidence for catalytic activity.
- Do not infer catalysis from structure prediction, confidence, or RMSD alone.
- When kinetics are available, distinguish `kcat`, `KM`, and `kcat/KM`; do not substitute one for another.
- Explain whether each state or intermediate in a multistep mechanism was modeled and how compatibility across states was assessed.

## Claims, terminology, figures, and limitations

### Design and prediction

- Call the final computational structure associated with a designed sequence the `design model` when applicable.
- Use `prediction` for outputs from a structure-prediction model used to evaluate a sequence.
- Compare an experimental structure with the design model and report the metric and aligned region.
- Do not say the prediction created the molecule or that structural agreement proves function.

### Figures

- Give every main figure one narrative job.
- For method papers, a frequent sequence is: conceptual method, benchmark/ablation, mechanistic or trajectory analysis, design cases, and experimental or downstream validation.
- Introduce the figure's question before listing panels.
- Make captions self-contained: define panels, colors, structures, sample sizes, denominators, error bars, statistics, and abbreviations.
- Use consistent semantic colors across figures and distinguish design models, predictions, experimental structures, and reference states visually.

### Limitations

Common limitations in the corpus include low design hit rates, dependence on hydrophobic target patches, weak transfer to new allomorphs or targets, incomplete structural validation, lower screening capacity than display methods, background activity, incomplete specificity, model or assay scope, safety, manufacturability, and lack of therapeutic readiness.

State the limitation next to the claim it constrains. Do not hide all limitations in one final caveat paragraph.

## How to use the manifest

Search `corpus-manifest.tsv` by year, slug, DOI, access, or version. Before citing a comparator:

1. Confirm `access` is `FULL_TEXT_READ` for section-level conclusions.
2. Check `version` before applying journal-format observations.
3. Prefer the version of record when refreshing a preprint record.
4. Reclassify only after the replacement full text has actually been inspected.
5. Record transient failures separately from permanent access status.

Never use Sci-Hub or another unauthorized mirror. Prefer publisher open access, IPD/Baker institutional copies, PubMed Central or Europe PMC, and institutional author manuscripts.
